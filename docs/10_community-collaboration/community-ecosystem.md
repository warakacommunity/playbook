# Community Ecosystems

African NLP is, to an unusual degree, a community effort. The datasets, models and benchmarks that move the field forward are built less by large institutions than by networks of volunteers, students and researchers who coordinate online across the continent.

The Masakhane community captures this spirit in its name, which means "we build together" in isiZulu. It holds that African language technology should be built by Africans, for Africans, with communities owning what they create ([Masakhane](../references.md#masakhane)). Knowing this ecosystem is practical knowledge: it is where you find collaborators, datasets, annotators and funding.

:::tip[Key points]

- African NLP is built mostly by distributed communities, not single institutions.
- Pan-African hubs, language groups, universities, companies and funders each play a different role.
- Good partnerships keep the community in control of its data, even when funding comes from outside.
- Make contributing easy and credit every contributor by name.
- Motivate volunteers without monitoring or public pressure.

:::

## The communities and what they do

The ecosystem has a recognisable shape. The list below is not exhaustive, and it grows every year.

| Group | Role |
|---|---|
| **Masakhane** | A pan-African grassroots research community whose participatory model produced foundational datasets such as MasakhaNER and MAFAND-MT ([Nekoto et al., 2020](../references.md#nekoto-2020); [Adelani et al., 2022a](../references.md#adelani-2022); [Adelani et al., 2022b](../references.md#mafand-2022)). |
| **Language and country groups** | Groups such as HausaNLP, EthioNLP, GhanaNLP and Digital Umuganda in Rwanda, with deep expertise in their own languages. |
| **Deep Learning Indaba** | Founded in 2017, the community's annual meeting place. Its local IndabaX events had reached 47 countries by 2024 to 2025, building the skills the field depends on ([Deep Learning Indaba](../references.md#deep-learning-indaba)). |
| **AI4D-Africa** | Funded and coordinated dataset creation through its African Language Program ([Siminyu et al., 2021](../references.md#siminyu-2021)). |
| **Zindi** | Hosts data-science competitions and a large community of African data scientists. |
| **Lelapa AI** | Builds African-centred language models, and co-developed the Esethu framework for community-centred dataset governance ([Esethu Framework, 2025](../references.md#esethu-2025)). |
| **Lanfrica** | Maps African language resources so they can be found ([Lanfrica](../references.md#lanfrica)). |

## Academic and industry collaboration

Good African-language datasets increasingly come from partnerships across academia, industry and communities. Each partner brings something different:

- **University centres** bring linguistic depth and continuity. Examples include the Data Science for Social Impact group at the University of Pretoria, the Maseno Centre for Applied AI in Kenya, and SADiLaR, South Africa's national language-resource infrastructure.
- **Companies and funders** bring scale and money. African Next Voices paired local centres with Gates Foundation funding to record thousands of hours of speech ([African Next Voices, 2025](../references.md#african-next-voices)), and Lacuna Fund supports dataset creation across the continent ([Lacuna Fund, n.d.](../references.md#lacuna-fund)).

The healthiest partnerships keep the community in control of the data, even when the money comes from outside. This is the central argument of the [data governance](../data-governance/index.md) chapter.

## Contributing, and crediting contributors

A community resource is only as strong as the people who contribute to it. Make contribution easy and make credit real:

- **Write clear contributor guidelines.** Say what help is wanted (writing, reviewing, sharing a dataset, translating) and how to get started.
- **Lower the barrier to a first contribution.** Many of the field's most valuable people began as students who fixed one small thing.
- **Name contributors** in datasheets, publications and release notes. Recognition is fair, and it is the cheapest way to keep a volunteer community alive.

The steady growth of African NLP research over the past two decades has gone hand in hand with this widening circle of contributors ([Belay et al., 2025](../references.md#belay-2025)).

### Motivate without surveillance

Recognition should make participation more visible and rewarding without turning the community into a surveillance system.

**Do:**

- Keep personal goals and streaks private by default.
- Make any leaderboard opt-in, use a chosen alias, and rank validated work rather than raw submissions.
- Count validation, review, mentoring and documentation as contributions, not only new labels.
- Prefer community-level progress towards shared language or coverage goals.

**Do not:**

- Publish personal totals without consent.
- Frame monitoring as motivation, or use rankings to pressure people into faster work.
- Send pushy re-engagement messages or penalise people for taking a break.
- Make public comparisons that expose contributors with low volumes or those working on sensitive material.
