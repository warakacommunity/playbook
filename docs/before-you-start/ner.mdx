# Named Entity Recognition

*Worked example. Last reviewed: 2026-07-07.*

This page applies the [four steps from Before You Start](./index.md) to named entity recognition (NER): finding people, organisations, places, and dates in text. It uses **MasakhaNER** as the example because it is the best-documented NER effort for African languages. If your language is not mentioned here, go back to [Step 1](./index.md#step-1-search-for-prior-work) and search.

## Step 1: What a search turns up

Search Hugging Face, Lanfrica, or the ACL Anthology for NER in almost any major African language and you will quickly reach two datasets:

- **[MasakhaNER 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.12391)** (Adelani et al., 2022): 20 languages. Bambara, Chichewa, chiShona, Ewe, Fon, Ghomala, Hausa, Igbo, isiXhosa, isiZulu, Kinyarwanda, Kiswahili, Luganda, Luo, Mossi, Nigerian Pidgin, Setswana, Twi, Wolof, and Yoruba.
- **[MasakhaNER 1](https://aclanthology.org/2021.tacl-1.66/)** (Adelani et al., 2021): the first 10 languages. Version 2 covers most of them, but version 1 is still the one to use for **Amharic**, which version 2 does not include. Its paper also explains how the annotation process was designed.

The data is on the [Hugging Face Hub](https://huggingface.co/datasets/masakhane/masakhaner2), and the [GitHub repository](https://github.com/masakhane-io/masakhane-ner) has the annotation guidelines and evaluation scripts.

You will also find **automatically labelled ("silver") NER data** that covers many more languages, built by projecting labels from Wikipedia links rather than by human annotators. It is tempting because of its coverage. Step 2 is where you find out whether its quality is good enough for your purpose.

## Step 2: Judging MasakhaNER 2 against the checklist

- **Licence.** CC BY-NC 4.0: free for research, **not for commercial use**. If you are building a product, check this first.
- **Access.** Openly downloadable from Hugging Face and GitHub.
- **Fit.** News text. Social media, government documents, or speech transcripts look different, so expect lower accuracy on them.
- **Script.** Latin script only. Hausa Ajami, Arabic-script Wolof, and Ge'ez-script languages are not covered.
- **Code-switching.** The sentences are monolingual. Real text often mixes languages within a sentence (Hausa–English, Swahili–English, French–Wolof). If yours does, expect results to be worse than the published numbers.
- **Labels.** Person, organisation, location, and date. "Organisation" may not match what your use case means by it, so read the guidelines before assuming the label set fits.
- **Splits.** Fixed train, development, and test splits, so results are comparable across papers.
- **Guidelines.** Released. Even if your language is not covered, the guidelines are the best starting point for writing your own.
- **Quality.** Annotated by native speakers through a participatory process. The papers describe it in detail.
- **Diacritics.** In Yoruba, Igbo, and several other languages, tone marks carry meaning. Check that your text processing keeps them, or entity boundaries will shift and results will look worse than they are.

## Step 3: Reuse, extend, or build

Find the row that matches your situation, then follow it across. The first question is whether your language is covered by MasakhaNER 2.

<div className="decision-table">

| What you found | Your choice | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Your language is **covered** by MasakhaNER 2, and its labels (person, organisation, location, date) are enough for your task | **Reuse MasakhaNER 2** | Do not build a new corpus for these labels. |
| Your language is **covered**, but you need **extra entity types** (money, products, culture-specific) | **Extend it** | Annotate the extra types on the existing splits and reuse the guidelines. Do not re-annotate person, organisation, and location; merge. |
| Your language is **not covered**, but it is **related** to one of the 20 (same family or script) | **Borrow, then build small** | Try [cross-language transfer](/cross-language-transfer) from the closest covered language, then build a small evaluation set in your own language using the MasakhaNER guidelines. |
| Your language is **not covered** and **not related** to any of the 20 | **Build from scratch** | Start with the [long-tail language onboarding](/long-tail-language) chapter, then [Data Collection](/data-collection/Overview), [Annotation Design](/annotation-design/annotation-task-design), and [Data Quality](/data-quality). Read the MasakhaNER guidelines before writing your own, and aim for 5,000–10,000 sentences to start. |

## Step 4: Learning from the people who built it

MasakhaNER was built by volunteer researchers and native-speaker annotators across Africa. Their way of organising the work is worth studying before you design your own project.

**Read first:**

- The **[MasakhaNER case study](../case-studies/masakhaner.md)** in this playbook: how the project was organised and what the team learned.
- The **[MasakhaNER 2 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.12391)** (Adelani et al., 2022): the 20-language dataset and its analysis.
- The **[MasakhaNER 1 paper](https://aclanthology.org/2021.tacl-1.66/)** (Adelani et al., 2021): how the annotation process was designed.
- The **[participatory research paper](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.195/)** (Nekoto et al., 2020): the Masakhane way of working that MasakhaNER grew out of.

**Questions to ask the authors:**

1. How did you recruit and coordinate annotators in 20 countries?
2. Where did annotators disagree most, and how did you resolve it?
3. What changed between version 1 and version 2, and why?

Once you have your data, training a model is well covered elsewhere. The [Hugging Face token classification tutorial](https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/tasks/token_classification) is a good start.
