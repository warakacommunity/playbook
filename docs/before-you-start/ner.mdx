# Named Entity Recognition

*Worked example. Last reviewed: 2026-07-07.*

This page applies the [four steps from Before You Start](./index.md) to named entity recognition (NER): finding people, organisations, places, and dates in text. It uses **MasakhaNER** as the example because it is the best-documented NER effort for African languages. **It is not a complete list.** If your language is not mentioned here, go back to [Step 1](./index.md#step-1-search-for-prior-work) and search.

## Step 1: What a search turns up

Search Hugging Face, Lanfrica, or the ACL Anthology for NER in almost any major African language and you will quickly reach two datasets:

- **[MasakhaNER 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.12391)** ([Adelani et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.12391)): 20 languages. Bambara, Chichewa, chiShona, Ewe, Fon, Ghomala, Hausa, Igbo, isiXhosa, isiZulu, Kinyarwanda, Kiswahili, Luganda, Luo, Mossi, Nigerian Pidgin, Setswana, Twi, Wolof, and Yoruba.
- **[MasakhaNER 1](https://aclanthology.org/2021.tacl-1.66/)** ([Adelani et al., 2021](https://aclanthology.org/2021.tacl-1.66/)): the first 10 languages. Version 2 covers most of them, but version 1 is still the one to use for **Amharic**, which version 2 does not include. Its paper also explains how the annotation process was designed.

The data is on the [Hugging Face Hub](https://huggingface.co/datasets/masakhane/masakhaner2), and the [GitHub repository](https://github.com/masakhane-io/masakhane-ner) has the annotation guidelines and evaluation scripts.

You will also find **automatically labelled ("silver") NER data** that covers many more languages, built by projecting labels from Wikipedia links rather than by human annotators. It is tempting because of its coverage. Step 2 is where you find out whether its quality is good enough for your purpose.

## Step 2: Judging MasakhaNER 2 against the checklist

- **Licence.** CC BY-NC 4.0: free for research, **not for commercial use**. If you are building a product, check this first.
- **Access.** Openly downloadable from Hugging Face and GitHub.
- **Fit.** News text. Social media, government documents, or speech transcripts look different, so expect lower accuracy on them.
- **Script.** Latin script only. Hausa Ajami, Arabic-script Wolof, and Ge'ez-script languages are not covered.
- **Code-switching.** The sentences are monolingual. Real text often mixes languages within a sentence (Hausa–English, Swahili–English, French–Wolof). If yours does, expect results to be worse than the published numbers.
- **Labels.** Person, organisation, location, and date. "Organisation" may not match what your use case means by it, so read the guidelines before assuming the label set fits.
- **Splits.** Fixed train, development, and test splits, so results are comparable across papers.
- **Guidelines.** Released. Even if your language is not covered, the guidelines are the best starting point for writing your own.
- **Quality.** Annotated by native speakers through a participatory process. The papers describe it in detail.
- **Diacritics.** In Yoruba, Igbo, and several other languages, tone marks carry meaning. Check that your text processing keeps them, or entity boundaries will shift and results will look worse than they are.

## Step 4: Learning from the people who built it

MasakhaNER was built by a distributed community of volunteer researchers and native-speaker annotators across Africa. How they organised that work is as useful as the data itself.

- Read the **[MasakhaNER case study](../case-studies/masakhaner.md)** in this playbook.
- Read the **[participatory research paper](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.195/)** ([Nekoto et al., 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.195/)) that MasakhaNER's way of working grew out of.
- Questions worth asking the authors: How did you recruit and coordinate annotators in 20 countries? Where did annotators disagree most, and how did you resolve it? What changed between version 1 and version 2, and why?

## What it will cost

These are rough estimates based on the MasakhaNER papers and the participatory workflow they describe. They will not be exact for your project. They are here so nobody plans NER for a new language as a two-week task.

- **Reusing MasakhaNER 2 for a covered language.** One to two person-weeks, mostly spent on evaluation.
- **Adding new entity types for a covered language.** Four to eight person-weeks per language: writing a guideline addendum, training annotators, and resolving disagreements.
- **Building a new corpus of 5,000–10,000 sentences.** Three to six months elapsed. One to three person-months of annotator work, plus about one person-month from a lead annotator for guidelines and adjudication. This assumes two to four native-speaker annotators and one senior annotator.
- **Reaching good inter-annotator agreement.** Plan one full round of recalibration after the first 500 sentences. Do not treat the first pass as final.

## Further reading

- [MasakhaNER 2 (Adelani et al., 2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.12391): the 20-language dataset and its analysis.
- [MasakhaNER 1 (Adelani et al., 2021)](https://aclanthology.org/2021.tacl-1.66/): the annotation method and participatory workflow.
- [Participatory research for low-resourced machine translation (Nekoto et al., 2020)](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.195/): the Masakhane way of working that MasakhaNER inherits.

Once you have your data, training a model is well covered elsewhere. The [Hugging Face token classification tutorial](https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/tasks/token_classification) is a good start.
