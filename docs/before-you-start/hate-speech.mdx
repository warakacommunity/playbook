# Hate speech and content safety

*Worked example. Last reviewed: 2026-07-07.*

This page applies the [four steps from Before You Start](./index.md) to hate speech and abusive language detection. It uses **AfriHate** as the example because it is the largest community-built hate speech collection for African languages, with native-speaker annotators and a published paper. **It is not a complete list.** Many smaller, single-language datasets exist, and new ones appear often. If your language is not mentioned here, go back to [Step 1](./index.md#step-1-search-for-prior-work) and search.

This task is different from the others in this chapter in three ways. Annotators read harmful content for hours, so they need support from the first day. A classifier used for automated moderation can cause the same harm it is meant to reduce. And what counts as hate is contested, culturally and politically. Plan, staff, and budget for all three, or choose a different task.

## Step 1: What a search turns up

Search Hugging Face, Lanfrica, or the ACL Anthology for hate speech in a major African language and you will find many small datasets, each with its own labels and guidelines. The one that brings most of them together is:

- **[AfriHate](https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.92/)** ([Muhammad et al., 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.92/)): tweets in 15 languages. Algerian Arabic, Amharic, Hausa, Igbo, Kinyarwanda, Moroccan Arabic, Nigerian Pidgin, Oromo, Somali, Swahili, Tigrinya, Twi, isiXhosa, Yoruba, and isiZulu. Each tweet is labelled hate, abusive, or normal, and hateful tweets also carry a target (ethnicity, politics, gender, disability, religion, or other).

The data is on the [Hugging Face Hub](https://huggingface.co/datasets/afrihate/afrihate). The [GitHub repository](https://github.com/AfriHate/AfriHate) lists the language coordinators, who are good people to contact.

You will also find datasets for **nearby tasks** that look useful: sentiment and emotion data (for example, the anger and disgust labels in [NaijaSenti](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.08277)), or polarization data such as [POLAR](https://aclanthology.org/2026.semeval-1.453/) (SemEval-2026 Task 9, which includes Amharic, Hausa, and Swahili). They are tempting because the content overlaps. But negative or polarizing text is not the same as hate speech, and the labels measure a different thing. The "Labels" check in Step 2 is where you catch this. Use such data as extra context, never as hate speech labels.

## Step 2: Judging AfriHate against the checklist

- **Licence.** The Hugging Face card lists Apache 2.0, which allows commercial use. The text is tweets, so also check whether the platform's terms affect what you plan to do.
- **Access.** Gated. You must log in to Hugging Face and agree to share your contact details before you can download it.
- **Fit.** Twitter/X posts from 2012 to 2023. Comments on other platforms, radio call-ins, WhatsApp messages, or forums look different. Hate speech also changes fast: new slurs, codewords, and spellings appear all the time, so older data misses them.
- **Script.** Mostly Latin script. Amharic and Tigrinya are in Ethiopic script, Algerian Arabic is in Arabic script, and Moroccan Arabic appears in both Arabic and Latin script.
- **Code-switching.** African-language hate speech often mixes in English, French, or Arabic, and the offensive part is often exactly where the switch happens. Check how much of your target text mixes languages, and include such text in your evaluation.
- **Labels.** Hate, abusive, and normal, plus a target for hateful tweets. What counts as hate is specific to a culture, a region, and a time: a word that is neutral in one place can carry a history of violence in another. Read the paper's definitions before assuming they match yours. Do not import a label scheme from an English corpus without consulting the affected communities.
- **Splits.** Fixed splits. Each test set has at least 100 examples of each class, so the rare hate class can be measured. Real content is mostly not hate, so also report results per class with the number of examples, not just one overall score.
- **Guidelines.** The paper describes the definitions, the keyword lists, and the annotation process. Check what has been released before planning to reuse them, and ask the authors if you cannot find the full guidelines.
- **Quality.** Annotated by native speakers from the local communities, with three or four annotators per tweet. Agreement is reported per language and ranges from 0.46 to 0.81 (Randolph's kappa), so quality varies by language. Look at the number for yours. The individual annotations are released, which helps if you want to study disagreement. This matters: annotators from outside the community label hate speech less accurately and with more bias ([Sap et al., 2019](https://aclanthology.org/P19-1163/)).
- **Documentation.** The paper has limitations and ethics sections. Tweets were found with keyword lists, so the data has selection bias: it only contains hate that matches the words the team searched for. The Twitter/X academic API used to collect it is no longer free, so extending the data the same way is harder today.

## Protecting annotators and users

**Annotators.** Reading hateful content causes real harm. This is well documented from content moderation work ([Roberts, 2019](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/9780300235883/behind-the-screen)). If you cannot fund the following, do not start the project:

- **Limited exposure:** no more than a few hours a day on harmful content, with required breaks.
- **Counselling:** a mental-health professional that annotators can reach in confidence.
- **Regular debriefs** where annotators can talk about the material without stigma.
- **Opt-out:** anyone can stop at any time, without penalty or explanation.
- **Fair pay** that reflects the harm of the work, not just the hours.
- **A named contact** other than the project lead, to whom annotators can raise concerns.

**Users.** A dataset that trains a hate speech classifier does not make that classifier a moderation system. The AfriHate authors state that models built on their data should not be used for automated removal, and that any deployed system should keep native or near-native speakers in the loop. Before any deployment, you need:

- a written moderation policy decided by the deploying organisation and its community, not left to the model;
- human review of borderline cases, and an appeal process for users;
- false-positive rates measured separately for each dialect, language variety, and community. A classifier that over-flags one group silences the people it was meant to protect;
- a plan to refresh the data as evasion patterns change.

Who is moderated, how, and with what appeal, is a policy decision. It needs community consultation, ethics review, and legal input. See the [legal, consent, and community IP](../legal-consent/index.md) chapter. If you cannot commit to this, use the output only as an advisory tool for human moderators.

## Step 4: Learning from the people who built it

AfriHate was built by language teams across Africa, each with a coordinator, working under a shared set of labels. Its hardest problems were finding hateful posts in languages the Twitter API did not support, and handling honest disagreement about what counts as hate.

- There is no case study for AfriHate in this playbook yet. If you work on it, the [retrospective template](../case-studies/retrospective-template.md) is a good way to share what you learn.
- Read the **[AfriHate paper](https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.92/)**, especially the data collection, limitations, and ethics sections.
- Read the **[participatory research paper](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.195/)** ([Nekoto et al., 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.195/)) on how to involve communities in dataset work. This matters more for label design here than for most tasks.
- Questions worth asking the authors: How did you build the keyword lists, and what hate did they miss? Where did annotators disagree most, and did disagreement follow region, age, or politics? What support did annotators need, and what would you add next time?

## What it will cost

Hate speech projects cost more than sentiment projects of the same size. The extra cost is annotator support, ethics review, external audit, and slower annotation. These are rough estimates, not exact figures for your project.

- **Reusing AfriHate for a covered language.** One to three person-weeks. This is the cheap part.
- **Building a new corpus of 5,000–10,000 labelled examples.** Six to twelve months elapsed. Four to twelve person-months of annotator work, plus one to two person-months of lead effort for guidelines, community consultation on labels, and adjudication. **Add 20–40% for annotator mental-health support, ethics review, and slower annotation**, because people cannot read this content at the speed they read sentiment data.
- **Audit before deployment.** If the work is heading toward automated moderation, budget at least as much time for external audit, false-positive checks on real user content, and community consultation as for building the classifier itself.
- **Keeping the data current.** Hate speech changes quickly: data from 2024 is out of date by 2026, and often wrong about new slurs and codewords. Budget for regular data refreshes, not a one-off release.

## Further reading

- [AfriHate (Muhammad et al., 2025)](https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.92/): the 15-language dataset, how it was collected, and its limitations.
- [Challenges and frontiers in abusive content detection (Vidgen et al., 2019)](https://aclanthology.org/W19-3509/): a survey of the method problems in this field. Read it before writing guidelines.
- [The risk of racial bias in hate speech detection (Sap et al., 2019)](https://aclanthology.org/P19-1163/): how annotator background creates biased labels.

Once you have your data, training a model is well covered elsewhere. The [Hugging Face sequence classification tutorial](https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/tasks/sequence_classification) is a good start.
