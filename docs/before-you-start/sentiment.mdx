# Sentiment analysis

*Worked example. Last reviewed: 2026-07-07.*

This page applies the [four steps from Before You Start](./index.md) to sentiment analysis: deciding whether a text is positive, negative, or neutral. It uses **AfriSenti** and **NaijaSenti** as the examples because they are the largest community-built sentiment datasets for African languages, annotated by native speakers. **It is not a complete list.** If your language is not mentioned here, go back to [Step 1](./index.md#step-1-search-for-prior-work) and search.

## Step 1: What a search turns up

Search Hugging Face, Lanfrica, or the ACL Anthology for sentiment in almost any major African language and you will quickly reach two datasets:

- **[AfriSenti](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08956)** ([Muhammad et al., 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.862/)): more than 110,000 tweets in 14 languages. Amharic, Algerian Arabic, Hausa, Igbo, Kinyarwanda, Moroccan Arabic, Mozambican Portuguese, Nigerian Pidgin, Oromo, Swahili, Tigrinya, Twi, Xitsonga, and Yoruba. It was also used in the [AfriSenti-SemEval 2023 shared task](https://afrisenti-semeval.github.io/), so many published systems report results on it.
- **[NaijaSenti](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.08277)** ([Muhammad et al., 2022](https://aclanthology.org/2022.lrec-1.63/)): the earlier Nigerian dataset that AfriSenti grew from. Hausa, Igbo, Nigerian Pidgin, and Yoruba, with around 30,000 tweets per language (about 14,000 for Nigerian Pidgin). Its paper also released sentiment lexicons.

AfriSenti is on the [Hugging Face Hub](https://huggingface.co/datasets/shmuhammad/AfriSenti-twitter-sentiment), and the [GitHub repository](https://github.com/afrisenti-semeval/afrisent-semeval-2023) has the data, the evaluation script, and individual annotator labels for several languages. NaijaSenti is on the Hub as [HausaNLP/NaijaSenti-Twitter](https://huggingface.co/datasets/HausaNLP/NaijaSenti-Twitter).

You will also find **copies of the same data** uploaded by other accounts, and small **sentiment datasets built by translating English data** or labelling tweets automatically with emojis or keywords. Copies are tempting because they load easily. Translated and auto-labelled data is tempting because it is cheap. Step 2 is where you check the licence of the copy you actually download, and whether auto-generated labels reflect how people in your language express feeling.

## Step 2: Judging AfriSenti against the checklist

- **Licence.** Check the copy you use. The main Hugging Face card says CC BY 4.0, but other copies on the Hub are tagged CC BY-NC-SA (non-commercial, share-alike). If you are building a product, confirm the licence with the authors first.
- **Access.** Openly downloadable from Hugging Face and GitHub.
- **Fit.** Twitter/X text only. It is short, informal, and full of abbreviations and hashtags. Customer service transcripts, health surveys, formal writing, or long comments look very different. Always build an evaluation set from your own target domain.
- **Culture.** What reads as negative in one culture can read as neutral or even polite in another. Indirect criticism is common in many African-language contexts and is often labelled neutral by annotators from outside the culture. Read the paper's annotation section before writing your own guidelines.
- **Code-switching.** Normal, not an edge case. Tweets in every covered language mix in English, French, Arabic, or a regional lingua franca. Your data will too.
- **Script.** Amharic and Tigrinya are in Ge'ez script. Algerian and Moroccan Arabic tweets may appear in Arabic or Latin script. Check that your text processing handles both.
- **Labels.** Three classes: positive, negative, neutral. Neutral is the hardest class. Sarcasm, mixed feelings, indirect expression, and plain facts all end up there. Report results per class, not only an overall score (see [core principles](../1_introduction/core-principles.md)).
- **Label scope.** Polarity is not emotion, stance, polarization, or harm. If you need emotion labels, see [emotion analysis](../11_text-classification/emotion-analysis.md). If you need content moderation, see [hate speech analysis](../11_text-classification/hate-speech-analysis.md). If you need to detect hostility between groups, look at polarization data such as [POLAR (SemEval-2026 Task 9)](https://aclanthology.org/2026.semeval-1.453/). Do not reuse AfriSenti labels for these tasks.
- **Splits.** Fixed train, development, and test splits for every language, so results are comparable across papers. The test sets have been public since 2023.
- **Guidelines.** Partial. The repository has a guidelines file for Algerian Arabic only. The paper describes the annotation process for each language.
- **Quality.** Annotated by native speakers. Individual annotator labels are released for several languages, so you can measure agreement yourself.
- **Privacy.** The tweets were anonymised: user mentions were replaced with `@user` and URLs were removed. If you collect new social media data, do the same, and check the platform's terms before you redistribute it.
- **Transfer.** Sentiment depends on culture-specific words and idioms. It transfers less well between languages than structural tasks such as NER. Measure transfer before you rely on it.

## Step 4: Learning from the people who built it

AfriSenti was built by native-speaker researchers and annotators in many African countries, working in a participatory way. There is no AfriSenti case study in this playbook yet. If you worked on it, the [retrospective template](../case-studies/retrospective-template.md) is the place to start one.

- Read the **[AfriSenti paper](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.862/)**, especially the section on the challenges of curating each language.
- Read the **[NaijaSenti paper](https://aclanthology.org/2022.lrec-1.63/)** for how tweets were collected, filtered, and labelled.
- Read the **[participatory research paper](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.195/)** ([Nekoto et al., 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.195/)) for the Masakhane way of working.
- Questions worth asking the authors: How did you decide what counts as neutral, and where did annotators disagree most? How did you find enough tweets in languages with little Twitter activity? What would you change about the guidelines if you started again?

## What it will cost

These are rough estimates based on the AfriSenti and NaijaSenti papers and the participatory workflow they describe. They will not be exact for your project.

- **Reusing AfriSenti for a covered language.** One to two person-weeks, mostly spent on evaluation and human review.
- **Extending AfriSenti with domain-specific data (health, market, customer service).** Two to four months elapsed. Two to five person-months of annotator work, plus one person-month from a lead annotator for guidelines and adjudication.
- **Building a new corpus of 5,000–10,000 examples for an uncovered language.** Three to seven months elapsed. Three to nine person-months of annotator work, plus one person-month from a lead annotator for guidelines. This assumes two to four native-speaker annotators. Twitter/X data is the cheapest to source. Verified in-domain data (transcribed voice notes, curated market speech) costs two to five times more per example.
- **Reaching good inter-annotator agreement.** Plan one full round of recalibration after the first 500 examples. Do not treat the first pass as final.

## Further reading

- [AfriSenti (Muhammad et al., 2023)](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.862/): the 14-language dataset, how it was collected, and the challenges per language.
- [NaijaSenti (Muhammad et al., 2022)](https://aclanthology.org/2022.lrec-1.63/): the four Nigerian languages, with collection, filtering, and labelling methods.
- [Quality at a glance (Kreutzer et al., 2022)](https://aclanthology.org/2022.tacl-1.4/): an audit of web-crawled low-resource data. Useful before you trust scraped or auto-labelled sentiment data.
- [Participatory research for low-resourced machine translation (Nekoto et al., 2020)](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.195/): the Masakhane way of working that AfriSenti inherits.

Once you have your data, training a model is well covered elsewhere. The [Hugging Face sequence classification tutorial](https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/tasks/sequence_classification) is a good start.
